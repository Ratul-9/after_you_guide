How to use this guide

The guide has four layers. You can stop after any of them.

  1. Part A teaches reinforcement learning (RL) from nothing: what an agent is, what a reward is, how a neural network learns to drive by trial and error, and every equation behind PPO, safe RL and multi-agent RL. If you have never heard the word "policy", start here.
  2. Part B teaches the traffic-engineering ideas the study borrows: TTC, PET, right of way, gap acceptance, turn arcs.
  3. Part C is the research itself: the question, the simulator, the observation, the reward, the safety cost, the ten methods, the scenes, the measures, the statistics, every result with its number, and the Phase 2 and Phase 3 plans.
  4. Part D explains why the results came out the way they did. It maps each reward and cost term onto the behaviour it produced, finds the root cause of freezing, crashing and the few conventions that emerged, and lists open questions and possible next steps.

At the end there is a glossary of every keyword (Part E), a set of practice questions (Part F) and the reference list (Part G).

Part AReinforcement learning from zero

A1. The idea in one paragraph

Reinforcement learning is learning by trial and error. An agent (here, a car's driving program) sits in an environment (here, a simulated city). At each moment it looks at what it can sense, picks an action (steer, accelerate, brake), and the environment answers with a new situation and a number called the reward that says how good that step was. Nobody tells the agent the correct action. It has to discover, by trying things many millions of times, which actions lead to more reward over the long run. The thing it learns is a policy: a rule that maps what it sees to what it does.

Compare this with the two other main kinds of machine learning:

  • Supervised learning gets labelled examples ("this image is a cat"). The right answer is given.
  • Unsupervised learning gets unlabelled data and finds structure in it.
  • Reinforcement learning gets only a score after acting, often delayed. The agent must work out which of its many earlier actions earned the score. This is the credit assignment problem, and it is the root of most of the difficulty in RL.

A3. Markov decision processes (MDPs)

The standard mathematical model of a single-agent RL problem is the Markov decision process, a tuple

$$\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma, \rho_0)$$

  • $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of states and $\mathcal{A}$ the set of actions.
  • $P(s' \mid s, a)$ is the transition probability: the chance of landing in state $s'$ after taking action $a$ in state $s$. In a simulator this is the physics engine.
  • $R(s, a)$ is the reward function, the expected reward for taking $a$ in $s$.
  • $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ is the discount factor (explained in A4).
  • $\rho_0$ is the distribution of starting states (where trips begin).

The Markov property says the future depends only on the present state and action, not on the full history:

$$P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, \dots) = P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t).$$

Partial observability (POMDPs)

A real car never sees the full state. It sees a lidar scan and some noisy messages from nearby cars. When the agent receives an observation $o_t \sim O(\cdot \mid s_t)$ instead of $s_t$ itself, the problem is a partially observable MDP (POMDP). Two consequences matter for this project:

  1. The best action may depend on the past (a car that was visible a second ago and is now hidden behind a building is still there). A plain feed-forward network has no memory, so it cannot use the past. Phase 2 adds a GRU memory for exactly this reason (A17 explains GRUs).
  2. Noise in the observation (the study adds Gaussian noise to the neighbour readings) makes the problem harder and more realistic.

A4. Return and discounting

The agent does not maximise the reward of one step. It maximises the return, the sum of future rewards:

$$G_t = r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k r_{t+k}.$$

The discount factor $\gamma$ does two jobs:

  • Mathematical: it keeps the infinite sum finite.
  • Behavioural: it sets the agent's horizon. A reward $k$ steps away is worth $\gamma^k$ as much as an immediate one. A rough rule is that the agent "cares" about the next $1/(1-\gamma)$ steps. With $\gamma = 0.99$ that is about 100 steps, which at 10 Hz is 10 seconds of driving.

The study's exact $\gamma$ is in the code repository and was not in the documents available to me. The idea still matters for Part D: the further away a reward is (such as the +10 arrival bonus at the end of a trip), the weaker its pull on the current action.

A5. Policies

A policy maps observations to actions.

  • Deterministic: $a = \mu(o)$.
  • Stochastic: $a \sim \pi(a \mid o)$, a probability distribution over actions.

For continuous actions such as steering, the usual choice, and the one used here, is a Gaussian (normal) policy. The network outputs a mean $\mu_\theta(o)$ for each action dimension, and the action is sampled as

$$a = \mu_\theta(o) + \sigma \odot \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).$$

Here $\theta$ are the network weights and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation (how much random variation is added). The probability density of an action is

$$\pi_\theta(a \mid o) = \prod_{d} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_d} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(a_d - \mu_{\theta,d}(o))^2}{2\sigma_d^2}\right).$$

Why randomness? During training, randomness is exploration: the agent tries actions slightly different from its current best guess, and some of those turn out better. Without exploration it would never discover improvements.

Two details from this project:

  • Noise floor. The study never lets $\sigma$ drop below 0.08 on throttle-brake and 0.02 on steering Paper. Without a floor, PPO can shrink $\sigma$ towards zero, exploration stops, and the policy can get stuck. Part D shows why this floor matters for the freezing result.
  • Testing with the mean. At test time the cars use $a = \mu_\theta(o)$, with no noise Paper. This measures what the policy has actually learned, without random wobble.

A6. Value functions, Q-functions and the advantage

To improve a policy you need to know how good things are. Three functions answer that.

State-value function, the expected return from observation/state $s$ if you follow $\pi$:

$$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[G_t \mid s_t = s\right].$$

Action-value (Q) function, the expected return if you take action $a$ first and then follow $\pi$:

$$Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a\right].$$

Advantage function, how much better action $a$ is than the policy's average behaviour in $s$:

$$A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - V^\pi(s).$$

A positive advantage means "this action was better than usual, do it more". A negative one means "worse than usual, do it less". Almost every modern policy-gradient method, PPO included, works by pushing up the probability of positive-advantage actions and pushing down negative ones. This is the single most important idea for understanding the study's Equation (3), where the reward advantage and the cost advantage compete.

The Bellman equations

Values satisfy a recursive consistency condition. The value now equals the immediate reward plus the discounted value of where you land:

$$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi,\, s'\sim P}\left[R(s,a) + \gamma V^\pi(s')\right],$$ $$Q^\pi(s,a) = R(s,a) + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P,\, a'\sim\pi}\left[Q^\pi(s',a')\right].$$

The optimal versions (for the best possible policy $\pi^*$) replace the average over $a$ with a maximum:

$$V^*(s) = \max_a \left[R(s,a) + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{s'}[V^*(s')]\right].$$

A7. Learning a value function: the critic

A neural network $V_\phi(s)$ with weights $\phi$ is trained to predict returns. This network is called the critic. Two ways to get a training target:

  • Monte Carlo: wait until the trip ends and use the actual return $G_t$. Unbiased but noisy.
  • Temporal difference (TD): use one real reward plus the critic's own guess for the next state. The TD error is

$$\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t).$$

$\delta_t$ is itself a one-step estimate of the advantage of $a_t$. TD targets have less noise but some bias, because they lean on the critic's possibly wrong guess.

The critic is trained by minimising the squared error

$$\mathcal{L}_V(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\left(V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{G}_t\right)^2\right],$$

where $\hat{G}_t$ is the chosen target.

In this project there are two critics for budgeted methods: a reward critic that predicts future reward and a cost critic that predicts future safety cost Paper. MAPPO-type methods give these critics extra information (Section A15).

A8. Generalised advantage estimation (GAE)

GAE blends one-step and many-step advantage estimates with a parameter $\lambda_{\text{GAE}} \in [0,1]$:

$$\hat{A}_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda_{\text{GAE}})^l\, \delta_{t+l}.$$

  • $\lambda_{\text{GAE}} = 0$ gives the one-step TD error (low noise, more bias).
  • $\lambda_{\text{GAE}} = 1$ gives the Monte Carlo advantage $G_t - V(s_t)$ (no bias, more noise).

PPO implementations normally use GAE. I do not have the study's $\lambda_{\text{GAE}}$ value. Do not confuse $\lambda_{\text{GAE}}$ with the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ of safe RL, which is a completely different thing and is the one the study talks about.

A9. The policy gradient

We want to change $\theta$ to increase the expected return $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[G_0]$. The policy gradient theorem gives

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\left[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\; A^{\pi}(s_t, a_t)\right].$$

In words: take the direction that makes action $a_t$ more likely ($\nabla \log \pi$) and scale it by how good the action was ($A$). Good actions get reinforced and bad ones suppressed. This is where the name reinforcement comes from.

  • REINFORCE is the oldest version. It uses the return $G_t$ instead of $A$, which is very noisy.
  • Subtracting a baseline $b(s)$ (usually $V(s)$) from $G_t$ does not change the expected gradient but cuts its variance a lot. $G_t - V(s_t)$ is exactly an advantage estimate.
  • An actor-critic method has an actor (the policy network) and a critic (the value network) trained together. PPO is an actor-critic method.

A10. Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)

Plain policy gradients are unstable: one large update can wreck a good policy, and since the policy then collects bad data, it may never recover. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) limits how far each update can move the policy.

Define the probability ratio between the new and old policy:

$$\rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}.$$

The clipped surrogate objective is

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;\; \operatorname{clip}\left(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right].$$

How to read it:

  • If $\hat{A}_t > 0$ (good action), the objective rises as $\rho_t$ rises, but only up to $1+\epsilon$. Beyond that the clip removes the incentive, so the policy cannot become too much more likely to take that action in one update.
  • If $\hat{A}_t < 0$ (bad action), the objective rises as $\rho_t$ falls, but only down to $1-\epsilon$.
  • $\epsilon$ is typically 0.1 to 0.3. (The study's value is in its repository.)

The full PPO loss usually combines three parts:

$$L(\theta,\phi) = -L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) + c_1\, \mathcal{L}_V(\phi) - c_2\, \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta],$$

where $\mathcal{H}$ is the policy's entropy (a bonus for staying random). This study instead keeps exploration alive with a hard noise floor on $\sigma$ (A5).

The PPO training loop:

  1. Run the current policy in the environment and collect a batch of steps (a rollout).
  2. Compute advantages (usually GAE) and returns.
  3. Standardise the advantages: $\hat{A} \leftarrow (\hat{A} - \text{mean}) / \text{std}$. This puts them on a fixed scale (mean 0, standard deviation 1) whatever the size of the raw reward. Remember this step: Part D shows it is central to the freezing result.
  4. For several epochs, split the batch into minibatches and take gradient steps on $L$.
  5. Throw the batch away and repeat. PPO is on-policy: it only learns from data collected by the current policy.

In the study, every one of the ten methods uses one and the same PPO trainer with the same hyperparameters Paper, so differences between methods come from the method, not from tuning.

Agent-steps. Training length is counted in agent-steps: one car taking one 0.1 s step is one agent-step. A run of 30 million agent-steps with 8 cars is 3.75 million environment steps of the whole scene, because each environment step produces 8 agent-steps. This matters for the "equal data" check in C12.

A11. Neural networks in one page

A neural network is a function built from layers. A fully connected (FC, or MLP) layer computes $h = f(Wx + b)$, where $W$ is a weight matrix, $b$ a bias vector and $f$ a non-linear activation such as $\tanh$ or ReLU ($\max(0,x)$). Stacking layers lets the network represent complicated functions.

Training means adjusting all weights to reduce a loss. The gradient of the loss with respect to every weight is computed by backpropagation (the chain rule applied layer by layer), and the weights move a small step against the gradient:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}.$$

$\eta$ is the learning rate. In practice an adaptive optimiser such as Adam is usually used. I do not have the study's network sizes or optimiser settings.

In this project the policy network takes the 175-number observation and outputs the mean of the Gaussian over the two actions. The critics take the same observation (or more, for central critics) and output one number each.

A12. Exploration, exploitation and local optima

  • Exploitation means doing what currently looks best.
  • Exploration means trying something else in case it is better.

Too little exploration and the agent settles into a local optimum, a behaviour that is better than all its small variations but far from the best. Freezing (standing still) is a local optimum in the study's budgeted runs: tiny changes (creeping forward a few centimetres) earn almost no progress reward but can raise cost, so the gradient points back to standing still. Part D develops this.

A13. Reward design, reward hacking and inaction

The reward is the only thing the agent optimises, so the agent does what the reward says, not what the designer meant. When those differ the agent finds loopholes. This is called reward hacking or specification gaming.

Classic patterns, all relevant to this study:

  • The do-nothing solution. If not acting avoids all penalties, an agent may learn to do nothing. The study's freezing is this pattern, caused by a cost budget rather than a reward.
  • Crash as an exit. If every step costs something (a time penalty) and crashing ends the episode without extra loss, crashing early can be cheaper than driving on. Knox et al. (2023) found that most published driving rewards they could evaluate prefer a crash to a car that never moves. Phase 2 of this project adds a term specifically to close this loophole.
  • Shaping. Extra rewards that guide learning (such as progress along the route) speed learning up but can create new loopholes if they are badly shaped.
  • Sparse versus dense rewards. A reward only at arrival is sparse and hard to learn from. A reward every step for progress is dense and easier. This study uses a dense progress reward plus sparse bonuses.

A14. Safe reinforcement learning

A14.1 Constrained MDPs

A constrained Markov decision process (CMDP) (Altman, 1999) adds a cost function $C(s,a) \ge 0$ and a limit $d$:

$$\max_\pi\; J_r(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t\Big] \quad \text{subject to} \quad J_c(\pi) \le d.$$

The cost limit can be written in different ways (Wachi et al., 2024):

Formulation Constraint Comment
Cumulative Total (discounted) cost over an episode $\le d$ Most common in benchmarks
Per step (average rate) Average cost per step $\le d$ Used in this study
Per distance Average cost per km $\le d$ Suggested by this study as the fix
Probabilistic Probability of any unsafe event $\le \delta$ Chance constraints

This choice is the most important design decision in the whole study (Part D, D2). A per-step limit divides cost by time. A car that does not move spends time without spending cost, so its average cost per step goes to zero.

A14.2 The Lagrangian method

Constrained problems are hard to optimise directly. The Lagrangian relaxation turns the constraint into a penalty with a weight $\lambda \ge 0$, called the Lagrange multiplier:

$$\mathcal{L}(\pi, \lambda) = J_r(\pi) - \lambda\,\big(J_c(\pi) - d\big).$$

Solve the min-max (saddle-point) problem

$$\min_{\lambda \ge 0}\; \max_\pi\; \mathcal{L}(\pi, \lambda).$$

  • Policy step (primal): improve $\pi$ on $J_r - \lambda J_c$. With advantages this means following $\hat{A}_r - \lambda \hat{A}_c$.
  • Multiplier step (dual): the derivative of $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to $\lambda$ is $-(J_c - d)$, so gradient descent on $\lambda$ gives

$$\lambda \leftarrow \max\big(0,\; \lambda + \eta_\lambda (J_c - d)\big).$$

If cost is above the budget, $\lambda$ grows and cost matters more. If cost is below, $\lambda$ shrinks. This is called dual ascent or gradient-ascent multiplier (it ascends the penalty), and together the two steps make a primal-dual method.

PPO-Lagrangian (Ray et al., 2019) is PPO with this multiplier. MAPPO-Lagrangian / MACPO (Gu et al., 2023) extend the idea to many agents, each with its own constraint.

A14.3 Problems with Lagrangian methods

  • Oscillation. $\lambda$ reacts to cost with a lag, so it overshoots and the policy swings between too careless and too careful.
  • PID Lagrangian (Stooke et al., 2020) treats $\lambda$ as the output of a PID controller on the constraint error $e_k$:

$$\lambda_k = \max\Big(0,\; K_P\, e_k + K_I \sum_{j\le k} e_j + K_D\,(e_k - e_{k-1})\Big).$$

Plain dual ascent is the integral term alone. The proportional term reacts immediately, and the derivative term damps overshoot. - Constrained Policy Optimisation (CPO) (Achiam et al., 2017) enforces the constraint inside a trust region instead of using a multiplier. - Reward Constrained Policy Optimisation (RCPO) (Tessler et al., 2019) is another Lagrangian approach. - Trivially safe policies. A policy that does nothing often meets a cost constraint perfectly. Safety Gym (Ray et al., 2019) designs its tasks so that reward and cost truly conflict, to rule this out. A 2024 review (Gu et al., 2024) calls staying in place for safety "extremely conservative". In robot navigation, the freezing robot problem (Trautman and Krause, 2010; Fan et al., 2019) is a robot that stops because every path looks unsafe.

A15. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL)

A15.1 Markov games

With several learning agents the MDP becomes a Markov game (or stochastic game):

$$\mathcal{G} = \big(N, \mathcal{S}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}_{i=1}^N, P, \{R_i\}_{i=1}^N, \gamma\big).$$

Each agent $i$ chooses $a_i$, the world moves according to the joint action $(a_1,\dots,a_N)$, and each agent gets its own reward $R_i$. When agents only see local observations it is a partially observable Markov game, or a Dec-POMDP if all rewards are equal.

In this study each car has its own reward (its own progress and arrival), so the game is general-sum: cars are neither purely cooperative nor purely competitive. Two cars at a junction both want to go first (competition) but both lose if they collide (common interest).

A15.2 The junction as a game

A two-car meeting at a junction is close to a classic game. Each car can Go or Yield:

Car B: Go Car B: Yield
Car A: Go both risk a crash (bad for both) A gains time, B loses a little
Car A: Yield B gains time, A loses a little both waste time (stand-off)

This is a version of the game of Chicken (or Hawk-Dove). It has two stable pure outcomes, A goes, B yields and B goes, A yields. Which one happens needs a way to break the symmetry. A convention is a shared rule that picks one, such as "the first to arrive goes" or "the car on the right goes". Without a convention, cars either both go (crash) or both wait (stand-off). The whole Phase 1 study is about whether learning fleets invent such conventions on their own.

A15.3 Conventions

Lewis (1969) defined a convention as a regularity in behaviour that each agent follows because the others follow it. If everyone else switched, you would switch too. Driving on the right is the textbook case. Conventions can emerge from adaptive play and repeated local interaction (Young, 1993; Shoham and Tennenholtz, 1997). Learned agents can also settle on arbitrary private conventions that fail with new partners (Lerer and Peysakhovich, 2019; Hu et al., 2020), which is why the study runs a cross-play test (C12).

A15.4 Non-stationarity and independent learners

If each agent learns its own policy and treats the others as part of the environment, these are independent learners (Tan, 1993). The trouble is that the others keep changing, so the environment each agent faces is non-stationary. The Markov assumption breaks, and what was a good action yesterday may be bad today (Matignon et al., 2012). Independent PPO nevertheless works well on some benchmarks (Schroeder de Witt et al., 2020). In this study, Direct PPO is exactly independent PPO.

A15.5 Parameter sharing

With parameter sharing (Gupta et al., 2017), all agents use one network. Each car still acts on its own observation, so cars can behave differently in different situations, but they all run the same function. Benefits:

  • Data. The network learns from every car's experience. With 8 cars it gets 8 times the data per environment step.
  • Consistency. Every car responds the same way to the same situation, so cars can predict each other. In effect each car plays against copies of itself (self-play).

The study separates these two benefits with an "equal data" check (C12).

A15.6 Centralised training with decentralised execution (CTDE) and MAPPO

In CTDE (Lowe et al., 2017), the critic may use extra information during training (other agents' observations, the global state), while each actor still acts on its own observation alone. The critic is not needed when driving, so this costs nothing at deployment.

MAPPO (Yu et al., 2022) is PPO with parameter sharing and a centralised critic. In this study the central critic is a mean-pooled critic: besides the car's own observation it gets the mean observation of the other cars in its group and their number, not the full global state Paper. MAPPO-L adds Lagrangian multipliers (Gu et al., 2023).

A15.7 Reward mixing and CoPO

Coordinated Policy Optimisation (CoPO) (Peng et al., 2021) gives each car a mixture of its own reward and its neighbours' rewards, controlled by a local coordination factor (LCF), an angle $\varphi$:

$$\tilde{r}_i = \cos(\varphi)\, r_i + \sin(\varphi)\, \bar{r}_{\mathcal{N}(i)},$$

where $\bar{r}_{\mathcal{N}(i)}$ is the mean reward of the neighbours. $\varphi = 0°$ is selfish, $\varphi = 45°$ weighs self and others equally, and $\varphi = 90°$ is fully altruistic. CoPO learns $\varphi$. This study's CoPO-style baseline fixes $\varphi = 45°$, and the mean is taken over the 8 cars of the car's operator group Paper. The paper says clearly that it draws no conclusion about CoPO itself.

A15.8 Self-play, cross-play and zero-shot coordination

  • Self-play: a fleet is tested with copies of its own cars.
  • Cross-play: cars from two different fleets (trained separately) are mixed. If each fleet invented a private convention, cross-play exposes it as a drop in performance.
  • Zero-shot coordination (Hu et al., 2020, "Other-Play") is the goal of coordinating with partners you never trained with.

A16. Curriculum learning and fine-tuning

A curriculum trains an agent on easy tasks first and harder ones later. The study's driving school is a seven-stage curriculum (A1 to A7) from a simple street to whole trips through a lights-off city, trained with one car alone. The result, the school graduate, is then fine-tuned in traffic: every fleet copies the graduate's weights and keeps learning. Fine-tuning from a common start has a big side effect that the study handles carefully: whatever the graduate already did will show up in every fleet, so a behaviour only counts as learned in traffic if the fleets differ from the graduate.

A17. Memory and perception networks (needed for Phase 2)

A17.1 Recurrent networks and the GRU

A recurrent neural network (RNN) keeps a hidden state $h_t$ that carries information from step to step. The gated recurrent unit (GRU) is a popular RNN cell:

$$z_t = \sigma(W_z x_t + U_z h_{t-1} + b_z) \quad \text{(update gate)}$$ $$r_t = \sigma(W_r x_t + U_r h_{t-1} + b_r) \quad \text{(reset gate)}$$ $$\tilde{h}_t = \tanh\big(W_h x_t + U_h (r_t \odot h_{t-1}) + b_h\big) \quad \text{(candidate)}$$ $$h_t = (1 - z_t)\odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t.$$

$\sigma$ here is the logistic sigmoid and $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication. The update gate decides how much of the old memory to keep, and the reset gate decides how much of it to use when forming new content. A car with a GRU can "remember" a car that has just disappeared behind a building.

A17.2 Encoders

An encoder compresses a large input (72 lidar beams, a camera image) into a small vector (an embedding). Three kinds appear in Phase 2:

  • Fully connected (FC): treats the input as a flat list. It has no built-in notion of which beam is next to which.
  • Convolutional (CNN): slides small filters along the input, so it uses neighbourhood structure (neighbouring beams look in neighbouring directions).
  • Transformer: uses self-attention, where each input element computes weights over all others: $\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\,V$.

An autoencoder trains an encoder together with a decoder that tries to rebuild the input from the embedding. The reconstruction error (mean squared error, MSE) measures how much information survives:

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2.$$

A frozen encoder is trained first and then fixed, so the policy learns on top of it without changing it.

A17.3 Lidar

Lidar sends out laser beams and measures the distance to the first thing each beam hits. Here a car has 72 beams reaching 50 m, one every 5 degrees around the car Derived: 360°/72. Phase 2's three-channel lidar reports separately whether a beam hit a moving object, the static scene, or a side boundary.

Part BTraffic engineering concepts used in the study

B1. Time to collision (TTC)

TTC (Hayward, 1972) is the time until two road users would collide if both kept their current speed and path. For a car following another on the same line,

$$\text{TTC} = \frac{\text{gap}}{v_{\text{follower}} - v_{\text{leader}}} \quad (\text{only defined when the follower is faster}).$$

In the study, $\text{TTC}_{ij}$ is the time to contact between cars $i$ and $j$ at current speeds along their routes Paper. Small TTC means danger is close. The study counts TTC below 1.5 s as a near miss.

B2. Post-encroachment time (PET)

PET (Allen et al., 1978) is used where two paths cross. It is the time between the first road user leaving the conflict point and the second road user arriving at it:

$$\text{PET} = t_{\text{second arrives}} - t_{\text{first leaves}}.$$

PET = 0 would be a collision. A small PET means the second car arrived just after the first had cleared, a near miss even though nothing touched. The study counts PET below 2 s as a near miss, and the median passing gap reported for negotiation is the PET.

TTC versus PET: TTC is a forecast measured continuously while cars approach. PET is measured once, after the event, from what actually happened.

B3. Near miss

A near miss (or traffic conflict) is an event that came close to a crash without one happening. Traffic engineers use near misses because they are far more common than crashes, so they give statistics faster. In the study a conflict ends as a near miss if the gap (PET) was under 2 s or TTC fell under 1.5 s.

B4. Right-of-way rules

At an uncontrolled junction (no lights, no signs), the California driver handbook says: the vehicle that arrives first goes first, and if two arrive together, the driver on the left yields to the driver on the right Paper. The study tests five rules, each named after the car it lets pass first:

Rule Where tested The car that passes first
FCFS (first come, first served) 4-way crossing The car that reached the conflict zone first
Right first 4-way crossing The car approaching from the right
Ring first Roundabout The car already on the ring (circulating)
Through first T-junction The car going straight along the main road
Mainline first On-ramp The car already on the highway, not the merging car

For every rule except FCFS, 50% means the rule plays no role: the passing order is a coin flip with respect to that rule.

B5. Gap acceptance and capacity

At priority junctions, a driver on the minor road waits for a gap in the main-road traffic big enough to enter safely. Gap acceptance models of capacity (Highway Capacity Manual, TRB 2022; roundabout guide, Rodegerdts et al. 2010) assume fixed priority rules. The study removes those rules, so this machinery does not apply directly. That is what makes the question new.

B6. The intelligent driver model (IDM)

IDM (Treiber et al., 2000) is a hand-written car-following rule: accelerate towards a desired speed, brake to keep a safe time gap to the car in front. It is the standard rule-based baseline in traffic simulation. The study notes as a limitation that it did not include an IDM fleet.

B7. Turning paths and the design arc

Every turn at a junction has a reference path: the centre line of the lane that connects the entry arm to the exit arm, usually an arc. The apex is the arc's midpoint. The study measures the signed offset of each car's path from the arc at the apex: negative means inside the turn (cutting the corner), positive means wide. Human turning paths also spread around the design arc, depending on junction shape and speed (Alhajyaseen et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2025).

C2. Motivation

Paper Junctions are where much of today's traffic danger lies. In US data from 1997 to 2004, slightly more than 20% of traffic deaths happened at intersections, most of them at junctions with stop signs or no traffic control at all (Subramanian and Lombardo, 2007). If every car drove itself, a city could in principle drop lights, signs and priority rules and let the cars settle who goes first. Those encounters would then be settled by learned driving policies rather than written rules.

C3. The research question

Paper Can a fleet of learning cars, in traffic without signals or priority rules, converge on a shared set of driving rules and habits that supports safe and reliable daily commuting, and how do the cars negotiate these rules among themselves?

It is split into three sub-questions:

  1. Negotiation. How do learning cars resolve a conflict? Does one yield and the other go, do both stop, or do they collide?
  2. Habits. Do they develop driving habits (such as how they take a turn), and do those resemble the way people drive?
  3. Safety tools. What do the two standard tools for making a learning fleet safe do to this behaviour? The tools are (a) a safety budget enforced by Lagrangian PPO, and (b) shared learning, one network driving all cars.

The answer, in one word, is "partly" (Section C14).

The ground rule of the whole study

Paper A behaviour counts as learned only if the trained fleets differ from the graduate they started from. Testing against chance (50%) is not enough, because the starting policy may already have had the behaviour.

C9. The safety cost (Equation 1)

Paper At every step, car $i$ pays

$$c_i = \sum_j \mathbb{1}^{\text{coll}}_{ij} + \mathbb{1}^{\text{road}}_{i} + 0.1 \sum_j \left(1 - \frac{\text{TTC}_{ij}}{1.5\,\text{s}}\right)_+ + 0.5 \sum_e \left(1 - \frac{\text{PET}_e}{2\,\text{s}}\right)_+ \tag{1}$$

  • $\mathbb{1}^{\text{coll}}_{ij}$ is 1 if car $i$ is in contact with car $j$, else 0.
  • $\mathbb{1}^{\text{road}}_i$ is 1 if car $i$ leaves the road, else 0.
  • $(x)_+ = \max(0, x)$, the "positive part".
  • $\text{TTC}_{ij}$ is the time to contact at current speeds along the routes. The term is non-zero only when TTC is under 1.5 s and grows linearly to 0.1 as TTC falls to 0.
  • $\text{PET}_e$ is the gap between the first car clearing the conflict point of event $e$ and the second reaching it. The term is non-zero only when PET is under 2 s and grows linearly to 0.5 as PET falls to 0.
  • Both cars pay every term. The PET term is charged once, when the event closes. The TTC term is charged every step for each neighbour under the threshold.

C10. The budget, the multiplier and the combined advantage

C10.1 The budget

Paper A budget $d$ caps the mean cost per car-step (one step is 0.1 s). "It is a limit per step, not per km, and this matters later: a car that stands still accrues almost no cost."

Level $d$ (cost per car-step) Fraction of the reference rate
Reference rate $3.16 \times 10^{-3}$ 1
PPO-Lag 1/2 $1.58 \times 10^{-3}$ 1/2
PPO-Lag 1/4 $0.79 \times 10^{-3}$ 1/4
PPO-Lag 1/10 $0.316 \times 10^{-3}$ 1/10

The reference rate is "a fixed design constant that is not calibrated per scene" Paper.

What these numbers mean in time Derived. A budget of $d$ allows on average one unit of cost every $1/d$ car-steps:

Level One unit of cost (e.g. one road departure) allowed every In driving time
Reference about 316 car-steps about 32 s
1/2 about 633 car-steps about 63 s
1/4 about 1,266 car-steps about 127 s
1/10 about 3,165 car-steps about 316 s (over 5 minutes)

Since a trip can last at most 100 s before it counts as stuck, the tight budgets allow less than one collision-sized cost per trip on average. A single PET near miss of 1 s (cost 0.25) uses up about 160 car-steps of the 1/2 budget.

Annealing. Paper Over the first 20% of training the effective budget $b$ falls geometrically from $3.16 \times 10^{-3}$ to $d$. A geometric schedule over training progress $u = t / (0.2\,T_{\text{train}})$ has the form

$$b(u) = 3.16\times 10^{-3} \cdot \left(\frac{d}{3.16 \times 10^{-3}}\right)^{u}, \quad 0 \le u \le 1, \qquad b = d \text{ afterwards}.$$

(This is the standard form of "falls geometrically". The code's exact step pattern may differ.) The schedule gives the fleet time to learn before the full constraint applies.

C10.2 Operators

Paper An operator is a group of car slots that shares one multiplier. A fleet has 8 operators, and car slot $j$ belongs to operator $j \bmod 8$. In an 8-car scene every car is its own operator, and in the 16-car grid each operator has two cars. Each operator has its own advantage normalisation and its own multiplier $\lambda_i$. Think of an operator as one company running some of the cars, with its own safety target.

C10.3 The multiplier update (Equation 2)

Paper $\lambda_i$ starts at 0. After each PPO update,

$$\lambda_i \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\left(\lambda_i + 0.05 \cdot \operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\bar{c}_i - b}{b},\; -1,\; 1\right),\; 0,\; 100\right) \tag{2}$$

where $\bar{c}_i$ is the operator's mean cost per car-step in the latest batch. Reading it:

  • $(\bar{c}_i - b)/b$ is the relative violation: +1 means cost is double the budget, -0.5 means cost is half the budget.
  • It is clipped to $[-1, 1]$, so $\lambda$ moves at most 0.05 per update in either direction.
  • $\lambda$ stays between 0 and 100.
  • This is dual ascent (A14.2) with a normalised, clipped step. The paper calls this the "gradient-ascent" multiplier rule, following Ray et al. (2019).

Derived Since each update moves $\lambda$ by at most 0.05, reaching $\lambda = 1$ takes at least 20 PPO updates of continuous violation, and coming back down from a value $\Lambda$ takes at least $\Lambda / 0.05$ updates of continuous under-spending.

C10.4 The combined advantage (Equation 3)

Paper The policy is trained on

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{\hat{A}_r - \lambda_i \hat{A}_c}{1 + \lambda_i} \tag{3}$$

where $\hat{A}_r$ and $\hat{A}_c$ are the standardised reward and cost advantages from separate critics. Rewrite it as weights:

$$\hat{A}_i = \underbrace{\frac{1}{1+\lambda_i}}_{w_r}\hat{A}_r \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{\lambda_i}{1+\lambda_i}}_{w_c}\hat{A}_c, \qquad w_r + w_c = 1.$$

$\lambda$ Weight on reward $w_r$ Weight on cost $w_c$ Meaning
0 1.00 0.00 Pure reward (like Direct PPO)
0.25 0.80 0.20 Mostly reward
1 0.50 0.50 Reward and cost count equally
4 0.20 0.80 Mostly cost
10 0.09 0.91 Almost only cost
100 0.01 0.99 Only cost

The division by $1+\lambda$ keeps the total size of the advantage roughly fixed, so a large $\lambda$ does not blow up the update. Keep the $\lambda = 1$ line in mind: the paper finds that every frozen run froze after the mean multiplier passed 1.

for each PPO iteration:
    run all cars for a batch of steps; record r_t and c_t for each car
    for each operator i:
        compute reward advantages A_r with the reward critic, standardise per operator
        compute cost advantages A_c with the cost critic, standardise per operator

    PPO clipped update of the policy on A_i  (one network per car, or one shared network)
    update reward critic and cost critic
    for each operator i:
        cbar_i = mean cost per car-step in this batch
        b = effective budget (annealing in the first 20% of training)

C11. The driving school and the school graduate

Paper A single car first learns to drive alone, in seven stages (A1 to A7) that end with whole trips through a lights-off city. Its final policy is the school graduate (fleet code grad). There is one graduate per seed.

  • Cost critics, central critics and multipliers start fresh, because the graduate never had them.
  • The graduate is also the baseline in every test: it is tested in exactly the same way as the trained fleets.
  • Paper The graduate "has never met another learning car". Whatever it does in traffic, it learned alone.

C14. Conflict events: when do two cars actually compete?

Paper To study negotiation you first need to know when two cars actually compete for the same piece of road.

Opening an event. An event opens when: 1. the planned 60 m paths of two cars come within 2 m of each other, 2. on different road links with no shared upstream link (so cars following each other, or splitting apart, do not count), and 3. both cars are 3 to 40 m from that point.

Closing an event. It closes when both cars have passed, when a trip ends first, or after 40 s.

Conflict zone. A car "reaches" the conflict zone within 15 m of the conflict point.

Merge contests (highway). A ramp car is paired with the nearest mainline car in the next lane, less than 20 m ahead or behind. The car that is ahead when the ramp car has merged passes first.

Recorded per event: order of reaching, order of passing, approach directions, ring or ramp position, PET, minimum TTC, any collision.

C17. Test protocol

Paper

  • Each fleet is tested on its own scene with the policy mean (no exploration noise). The graduate is tested the same way.
  • Main tests use the training volume: 8 cars, or 16 in the grid.
  • Arriving cars are replaced at once and failed cars after 1 s, so the number of cars is constant for the first 100 s of an episode. The episode then runs until the last trip ends.
  • Which data count where: trip outcomes count every trip; per-step cost and conflict events count only the first 100 s; right-of-way shares use all episodes.
  • Volume tests: 1, 2, 4, 8 and 12 cars in the unit scenes; 1, 8, 16, 24, 32 and 48 in the grid. (The density table also includes the densest traffic each map holds, up to six times the training volume.)
  • Alone versus in traffic: each fleet is also tested with one car, to check whether stopping is a reaction to other cars.

C18. Statistics, explained from scratch

Paper "Our statistical unit is the fleet, not the trip." Trips within one fleet are not independent (they share one policy), so treating 10,000 trips as 10,000 independent samples would make intervals far too narrow. Rates are computed per fleet and then averaged over the 5 fleets.

C18.1 Bootstrap confidence intervals

The bootstrap estimates how uncertain a statistic is by resampling the data with replacement many times and recomputing the statistic each time. A 95% interval is the range between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those recomputed values. Here the fleets are resampled (Agarwal et al., 2021). With only 5 fleets the paper notes the intervals "are approximate and may be too narrow".

Stratified bootstrap. When combining scenes, fleets are resampled within each scene, so every resample still contains every scene.

C18.2 Geometric mean of ratios

To summarise a method over seven scenes, the paper takes each scene's ratio to Direct PPO (for example crashes per km) and then the geometric mean:

$$\text{GM} = \left(\prod_{k=1}^{7} \text{ratio}_k\right)^{1/7} = \exp\left(\frac{1}{7}\sum_k \ln \text{ratio}_k\right).$$

Ratios are multiplicative, so a ratio of 2 and a ratio of 0.5 should cancel to 1. The geometric mean does that, whereas the ordinary mean would give 1.25. It also stops one extreme scene from dominating.

C18.3 Permutation tests

A permutation test asks: if the method label made no difference, how often would shuffling the labels give a difference at least as large as the one observed? The p-value is that fraction.

  • Stratified permutation test: method labels are shuffled only among the fleets of the same scene.
  • Exact two-sided test for 5 vs 5 fleets: every way of splitting 10 fleets into two groups of 5 is tried. There are $\binom{10}{5} = 252$ splits Derived, so the smallest possible two-sided p-value is $2/252 \approx 0.008$, which matches the paper's "smallest possible p 0.008".

C18.4 Multiple comparisons and Holm correction

Testing many hypotheses at the 5% level makes some false positives almost certain. With 40 tests you would expect about 2 false "discoveries" by chance alone. The Holm-Bonferroni method controls the chance of any false positive (the family-wise error rate) at 5%:

  1. Sort the $m$ p-values: $p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}$.
  2. For $k = 1, 2, \dots$, reject hypothesis $(k)$ if $p_{(k)} \le \alpha / (m - k + 1)$.
  3. Stop at the first one that fails; keep all the rest.

The paper uses Holm over 9 comparisons (each method against Direct PPO) and over the 40 rule cells.

C18.5 Chi-squared homogeneity test

A $\chi^2$ homogeneity test checks whether several groups (here the 5 fleets of one method) share one underlying rate:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{cells}} \frac{(\text{observed} - \text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}}.$$

A large $p$ means no evidence that the fleets differ.

C18.6 Lin's concordance correlation coefficient

Lin's concordance measures how close pairs of values are to being equal, not just correlated:

$$\rho_c = \frac{2\,\rho\,\sigma_x\sigma_y}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + (\mu_x - \mu_y)^2}.$$

It is 1 only if every point lies on the line $y = x$. The paper uses it to compare stuck share alone with stuck share in traffic per budgeted fleet, and finds 0.96, so cars get stuck about equally often in both conditions.

C18.7 Phase 2 statistics (planned)

  • Paired t-test: compares two conditions measured on the same units (for example the same seeds under two personalities). It uses $t = \bar{D} / (s_D/\sqrt{n})$, where $D$ is the per-pair difference.
  • TOST (two one-sided tests): tests equivalence, that a difference lies inside a margin $\pm\Delta$. Run two one-sided tests ($H_0: \mu_D \le -\Delta$ and $H_0: \mu_D \ge \Delta$). Equivalence is shown if both are rejected. Useful for claims such as "the encoder is as good as the raw scan".
  • IQM (interquartile mean): the mean of the middle 50% of values, recommended by Agarwal et al. (2021) as a robust summary of RL results.
  • $R^2$ (coefficient of determination): the share of variance in a target explained by a predictor, $R^2 = 1 - \sum (y - \hat{y})^2 / \sum (y - \bar{y})^2$.

C20. The paper's own conclusions

Paper

  1. Answer: partly. The cars converge on a shared way of negotiating (one yields, the other follows a few seconds later; conflicts end more cleanly; fleets trained apart stay compatible), not on a rulebook.
  2. Few explicit rules. Most priority behaviour comes from the starting policy. The one new rule is through-first at the T-junction under MAPPO-L, and the right-hand rule never appears. Trained cars also cut the left turn.

  1. Report the share of agents that end stuck next to cost and return, since a budget met by not moving looks like success on both.
  2. Test each policy alone as well as in traffic, since freezing without other agents is not caution towards them.
  3. Compare emergent behaviour with the starting policy, on contested events and not only against chance.

C21.4 Phase 2 equations Draft

Personality reward (Equation 4). A personality $p$ is a weight vector $\mathbf{w}_p$ over reward components $\varphi_k$ such as progress, speed, gap keeping and comfort:

$$r_t^{(p)} = \sum_k w_{p,k}\, \varphi_k(s_t, a_t). \tag{4}$$

Progress term (Equation 5). Because the weights alone let policies freeze (smoke test S8), a progress term pays 2.0 over a complete route, in proportion to the distance $\Delta s_t$ covered along a route of length $L$:

$$r_t^{\text{prog}} = 2.0 \cdot \frac{\Delta s_t}{L}. \tag{5}$$

Summed over a whole route, $\sum_t \Delta s_t = L$, so the total is exactly 2.0.

Crash time penalty (Equation 6). A crash at step $t_c$ pays the time penalty of the steps it skips up to the trip limit $T$:

$$r^{\text{crash}} = -c_{\text{time}} \cdot (T - t_c). \tag{6}$$

So crashing is never cheaper than driving on (Part D, D3, explains why this matters).

Scale-free PID multiplier (Equations 7 and 8). The original PID gains made the multiplier swing by $\pm 1$. The error now divides the cost excess by the budget $d$:

$$e_k = \frac{\hat{J}_{c,k} - d}{d}, \tag{7}$$

$$\lambda_k = \max\left(0,\; K_P e_k + K_I \sum_{j \le k} e_j + K_D (e_k - e_{k-1})\right). \tag{8}$$

$\hat{J}_{c,k}$ is the estimated cost at update $k$. Dividing by $d$ makes the error a relative violation, so the same gains work for any budget size. That is what "scale-free" means. The values of $K_P$, $K_I$, $K_D$ are not in the documents I had.

D2.2 The arithmetic of a per-step budget Derived

Write the cost per step as cost per distance times distance per step:

$$\frac{\text{cost}}{\text{step}} = \frac{\text{cost}}{\text{km}} \times \frac{\text{km}}{\text{step}}.$$

A car moving at $v$ m/s covers $0.1v$ m per step. As $v \to 0$, the second factor goes to zero, so the per-step cost goes to zero whatever the car's cost per km is. A frozen car is "infinitely safe" under a per-step budget. It does not need to drive any better.

The data confirm it from both sides:

  • 92 to 97% of stuck trips in traffic have zero cost, and a stuck PPO-Lag trip moves a median 0.8 m.
  • 43 of the 44 runs that met the budget had frozen. Meeting the budget and freezing are almost the same event.
  • Per km, the budgeted fleets cost 1.62, 1.52 and 1.82 times more than Direct PPO. They drove worse per distance, not better.

D2.3 Why the multiplier tips the policy into freezing Analysis

Follow one independent car through training.

  1. Early training. $\lambda = 0$, the car is a copy of the graduate and drives. The graduate crashes often in traffic (crash ratio 1.35, contact in 16% of conflicts), so cost is well above the budget.
  2. $\lambda$ climbs. Equation (2) raises $\lambda$ by up to 0.05 per update while cost exceeds $b$. Meanwhile the budget itself is tightening (annealing over the first 20% of training).
  3. The weights flip at $\lambda = 1$. By Equation (3), at $\lambda = 1$ reward and cost count equally, and above it cost counts more. This lines up with the paper's finding that every frozen run froze after the mean multiplier passed 1. PPO-Lag 1/2 freezes at a median of 13M steps.
  4. Which actions reduce cost? Two families: drive more carefully (keep larger gaps, yield earlier, never get TTC under 1.5 s) or slow down and stop. Careful driving is a hard skill that depends on reading other cars. Slowing down is easy: one throttle-brake output, the same in every state, that lowers TTC terms, prevents PET events and prevents collisions. Gradient methods follow the easiest direction of improvement, so they slide into slowing down.
  5. Standardised advantages magnify small cost differences. Each critic's advantages are standardised to unit variance (A10, step 3; C10.4). So even when the absolute cost differences between actions are tiny (a cost of order $10^{-3}$ per step), after standardisation they are as large in the update as the reward advantages. Once $\lambda > 1$ they also get more weight. The reward signal does not get a "size advantage" just because rewards are numerically larger than costs.
  6. Freezing is a sticky local optimum. Once stopped:
  7. Cost is about 0, below the budget, so $\lambda$ falls, but at most 0.05 per update. If $\lambda$ had climbed to, say, 5, it needs at least 100 updates of under-spending to fall back below 1 Derived from Equation 2.
  8. To discover that driving pays, the car must move. With the throttle-brake noise floor at 0.08 around a mean that says "brake", random exploration produces only tiny creeps, which earn tiny progress and still carry some risk. The reward advantage of "creep a little" is weak, so the gradient keeps pointing at "stay".
  9. The time penalty (-0.05 per step) does keep pushing towards moving, but in training a stuck car times out after 30 s, so the penalty is bounded, and its weight is only $1/(1+\lambda)$.

Evidence for the exploration part: raising the noise floor from 0.08 to 0.20 (together with scene-scaled budgets) cut stuck trips at the 1/2 level from 36% to 13% Paper. More exploration helps cars escape the frozen state when the budget is not too tight. At 1/4 and 1/10 it did not help (48% and 62% stuck), because there the budget is so tight that almost any driving violates it, so standing still really is the best feasible solution.

Evidence that the size of $\lambda$ is what matters: the fixed-penalty methods form a natural dose-response test Analysis. Fixed $\lambda = 1$ (cost weight 0.5) gives 1% stuck and 69% arrival, close to Direct PPO. Fixed $\lambda = 4$ (cost weight 0.8) gives 9% stuck, 54% arrival, and the second car waits a median 6.3 s at the crossing. The more weight on cost, the more stopping, with no multiplier dynamics involved at all.

D2.4 Why frozen cars freeze even when alone Analysis

Lin's concordance of 0.96 between stuck share alone and in traffic means the frozen policy does not condition its stopping on other cars. It has learned "do not move" as a near-constant output, not "wait while someone is crossing". That is the signature of a degenerate solution, not of caution. A cautious policy would stop more in traffic than alone. It also explains why PPO-Lag 1/2 ranks last in the density table already with one car (63% arrival alone in unit scenes, 26% alone in the grid).

D2.5 Why some budgeted runs kept driving Analysis

26 of the 61 runs that missed the budget kept driving, mostly at the on-ramp (14) and the diamond (7) Paper. At highway scenes, stopping is not "safe": a car stopped on an acceleration lane or a ramp still gets merged into, and cars coming from behind create small TTC values. So stopping does not reduce cost reliably there, the gradient no longer leads to freezing, and "the budget raises cost and road departures instead" Paper.

D2.6 The fix the study points to

Paper A per-distance budget with a guard against not moving. Analysis Per km, a frozen car's cost is undefined or infinite rather than zero, so the loophole closes. A guard (for example a cost or a reward penalty for time spent stuck, or a minimum progress constraint) makes inaction explicitly costly. Phase 2's progress term and crash time penalty attack the same loophole on the reward side.

D3. Root cause of Direct PPO's crashes

Paper Direct PPO crashes in 25% of trips. The reward has no collision term: a crash only ends the trip and forfeits the +10 arrival bonus.

Analysis Look at what a crash costs a car under this reward, compared with being careful:

  • Careful option (yield). Wait a few seconds. Each 0.1 s step costs -0.05, so waiting 3 s costs 1.5 in time penalty Derived, then the car goes on to collect the rest of its progress and the +10.
  • Aggressive option (go first). No waiting. Most of the time the other car yields and you save the 1.5. Sometimes you crash: you lose the +10 and the remaining progress, but you also stop paying the time penalty, because the trip is over.
  • With discounting (A4), the +10 lies at the end of the trip and is worth less at the moment of decision than the immediate time saved.

So the reward only weakly discourages risk. Crashing has no specific penalty, only an opportunity cost. This is exactly the misdesign Knox et al. (2023) describe: rewards under which a crash can be preferable to not moving. Phase 2's Equation (6) fixes this directly by making a crash at step $t_c$ pay the time penalty for all the skipped steps up to $T$. Crashing then always costs at least as much time penalty as staying in the trip would have.

Why do independent cars learn this risk-taking more than shared ones? See D4.

Why the graduate crashes even more (crash ratio 1.35): the graduate learned alone and never met another learning car. Many of its crashes come from situations it never trained on. Training in traffic (any method) gives the cars experience of conflicts, and conflicts end more cleanly afterwards (32% to 40-53% clean).

D4. Why shared networks drive better and never freeze

D4.1 What the paper establishes

  • Shared fleets lead on arrival (87 to 91%) and crashes (0.29 to 0.40 of Direct PPO) Paper.
  • Not only data: at equal data per network they still lead (79% vs 70%), and Direct PPO at 240M steps does not catch up Paper.
  • Not the central critic: a shared network with a local critic and the same budget behaves like MAPPO-L (89% vs 91%, no freezing) Paper.
  • Not a slack budget: MAPPO-L's multiplier binds (2.8 at the crossing, 3.7 at the T-junction), above the $\lambda = 1$ line where independent cars froze, and still no run freezes Paper.

D4.2 Possible mechanisms Analysis, not tested in the study

  1. Consistency removes non-stationarity. Under sharing every car runs the same function. When a car meets another car, it meets a copy of itself. The other driver's behaviour is predictable, and it changes in lock-step with its own learning. Independent cars face eight different, separately drifting partners, a moving target (A15.4). In the junction game (A15.2), a shared policy can settle on one consistent symmetry-breaking rule at once. Independent policies must each learn to read the others' changing habits.
  2. A shared policy cannot cheaply freeze for only some cars. Freezing one car of a shared network means freezing all of them in similar states. The shared gradient is a sum over 8 operators, each with its own $\lambda_i$ and advantage normalisation. Operators whose multipliers are low, or whose cars are making progress, pull the shared weights towards driving. An independent network has no such counterweight: its one car's gradient alone decides.
  3. Better driving lowers cost the honest way. Because shared fleets negotiate more cleanly (53% clean, 7% contact for MAPPO-L), they can meet the budget by driving well. MAPPO-L meets it in 21 of 21 runs with 0% stuck. When careful driving is reachable, the gradient does not need the frozen shortcut.
  4. More data per update gives less noisy advantages. Even at equal total data, sharing averages each update over many cars' situations, which can reduce the variance of the reward advantage so that the driving signal is not drowned out by the cost signal.

What would test these: train a shared network where each car has its own copy of the shared weights after initialisation (data and consistency separated); measure how $\lambda_i$ differs across operators in shared runs; or give independent cars a population of frozen partner policies to remove non-stationarity.

D4.3 Why the full city looks different Analysis

In the full city MAPPO-L's multiplier stays near zero, so the budget never binds Paper. With 24 cars spread over 40 junctions, conflicts per car-step are rarer and the 1/2 budget is easy to meet without changing behaviour, so MAPPO-L is close to plain shared learning there. One of its three fleets reached only 55%, which with 3 fleets is a wide spread. Direct PPO's drop below its own graduate (60% vs 80%) fits the non-stationarity story: in the city each independent car meets many changing partners, and independent fine-tuning made its starting skill worse.

D5. Why CoPO-style freezes without any budget Analysis

CoPO-style freezes in 31 of 35 runs with no cost at all Paper. The training signal for car $i$ is

$$\tilde{r}_i = \cos 45°\, r_i + \sin 45°\, \bar{r}_{-i} \approx 0.707\, r_i + 0.707\, \bar{r}_{-i}.$$

Possible mechanisms:

  • Diluted own credit. Half the signal is the others' mean reward, which the car's own action barely affects. That half is noise from the car's point of view, so the useful gradient on its own driving is weaker.
  • Waiting is rewarded. If car $i$ waits, other cars pass more easily and collect progress. Car $i$ gets 0.707 of their gains while paying only 0.707 of its own time penalty. Yielding becomes attractive, and since all cars share one network, they can all learn to yield, which ends in stand-offs and stuck trips (34% stuck).
  • Fixed, very prosocial angle. 45° weighs self and others equally. The original CoPO learns the angle locally, typically ending well short of fully prosocial. The fixed choice is a strong one, and the paper explicitly draws no conclusion about real CoPO.

Consequence: sharing alone does not prevent freezing. Sharing without reward mixing does.

D6. Why so few conventions emerged

D6.1 FCFS is inherited, and probably cheap Analysis on Paper facts

  • The graduate already does FCFS in 97.0% of crossing events. It learned to drive alone, so FCFS did not come from negotiation.
  • Kinematics: "the first car often clears the zone before the other arrives" Paper. In most events the arrival order is clear and the later car would have to brake anyway.
  • The observation hands it over: each car sees its neighbours' time to the next conflict site Paper. A car can compare that with its own and know who arrives first. "Go if I am first, wait if I am second" is a simple function of the observation. Analysis This privileged V2V cue may explain much of the clean negotiation, and the team flagged it as a likely reviewer question. Removing or degrading the cue in an ablation would measure how much.

D6.2 Right-hand priority stays at chance Analysis

  • No incentive. Nothing in the reward favours the car from the right. A rule is only needed to break ties, and ties are rare because FCFS settles most events.
  • Any tie-breaker would do. Even in contested events, the reward is symmetric: "right first" and "left first" are equally good conventions. Learning has no reason to pick the human one. The data even lean slightly towards left-first in contested events (30 to 49% right-first).
  • Too few contested events to shape a tie-break rule strongly.

D6.3 Through-first at the T-junction PaperAnalysis

The paper offers two readings: a learned minor-road give-way rule, or kinematics (the through car does not need to slow for a turn). Analysis Three facts fit together:

  1. In contested events all four main methods go through-first 81 to 96% of the time, against 73% for a small graduate sample (15 events). The kinematic advantage seems general.
  2. MAPPO-L is the only method where it clears the bar overall, and MAPPO-L's budget binds at this scene ($\lambda \approx 3.7$). A binding cost on near misses makes turning cars that would cut in front of a through car pay for it, so the cost reinforces the kinematic tendency.
  3. Turning cars are slower and the reward caps progress at the speed limit, so a turning car loses less by waiting than a through car would.

Whether the opposite rule could also hold was not tested, so it is not yet a convention in Lewis's sense.

D6.4 Cross-play compatibility Analysis

All fleets of a method start from the same graduate for a given seed, and different seeds' graduates were trained the same way. Their negotiation style (FCFS from the observation, yielding at a few seconds' gap) is largely the graduate's. Fine-tuning only nudges it. Mixing fleets therefore costs little. The one clear loss (MAPPO at the crossing, -4.5 points) suggests MAPPO fleets there drifted further from the common start, perhaps through the central critic. A test with graduates trained differently (or from scratch) would separate "learned compatible conventions" from "inherited the same habits".

D7. Why trained cars cut the left turn Analysis

The reward pays progress along the route and charges 0.05 per step. A left turn (in right-hand traffic) is the long turn, crossing the junction on a wide arc. Cutting inside the arc:

  • shortens the path, so fewer steps are spent and less time penalty is paid;
  • lets the car hold its speed, which is the reason the paper itself suggests, and progress is capped by the speed limit, so a shorter path at the same speed means an earlier arrival;
  • costs nothing in the reward unless the car crosses into the wrong side long enough (3 s) to count as "out", or hits something.

Right turns are short and tight already, so there is little to gain (within 0.18 m). Straight passes have nothing to cut (within 0.06 m). The effect is clearest at the diamond and the grid, where turns are numerous or long. The graduate learned alone, possibly without enough junction turns under time pressure to exploit this, so training in traffic adds it. Humans also cut corners for similar reasons, but the paper only claims a resemblance of kind.

D8. Why everything degrades in denser traffic Analysis

  1. Out of distribution. No method was trained above its training volume Paper. RL policies generalise poorly outside their training distribution.
  2. The observation sees only 4 neighbours. With 24 or 96 cars nearby, many relevant cars are invisible to the policy except through lidar. Analysis This is a hard ceiling on how well any of these policies can do in dense traffic.
  3. Interactions grow faster than cars. The number of potential conflicting pairs grows roughly with the square of the number of cars, so conflicts per car grow with density.
  4. The ranking holds because the reasons for it (consistency, no freezing) do not depend on density. PPO-Lag 1/2 is last even with one car because it is frozen, not cautious.

D9. Why the graduate is both the hero and the confound

The graduate design is the study's strongest methodological idea and also its main limit.

  • Strength: every method starts from the same driver, so differences are due to the method. And comparing to the graduate stops inherited behaviour from being mistaken for emergence (18 of 40 "conventions" against chance become 1 of 40 against the graduate).
  • Limit: the study shows what fine-tuning in traffic changes, not what learning from scratch would produce. Conventions might look different with no shared start. The paper lists this scope in its discussion.

D10. Open questions and likely reviewer points

From the paper's limitations and the team's notes:

  1. How much negotiation comes from the privileged V2V arrival-order cue? An ablation without the neighbours' time-to-conflict input would answer it.
  2. Untuned baselines. All methods share one set of PPO hyperparameters. A tuned Direct PPO might close some of the gap.
  3. Per-step vs per-km budgets. Only per-step budgets were trained. The per-km numbers (1.62, 1.52, 1.82) are computed after the fact.
  4. Only one multiplier rule in the main study (gradient ascent), plus a 9-run PID check.
  5. Few fleets. 5 per method (3 for checks), so intervals are approximate and the through-first result is a candidate pattern.
  6. One simulator, no pedestrians, crash rates far above human ones.
  7. Is "sharing prevents freezing" just a slack budget? Partly answered: MAPPO-L's multiplier binds at 2.8 and 3.7 without freezing.

D11. Possibilities: where this research can go

Stated in the paper or report:

  • A per-distance budget with an inaction guard.
  • A budget tight enough to bind in the full city.
  • Phase 2: controllable personalities (H5), no freezing under the new reward (H6), occlusion and memory (H7), encoders (H8), mixed-personality fleets (H9).
  • Phase 3: an application for running and inspecting agents; an open-source toolkit for uncontrolled scenarios.

Further ideas suggested by this analysis Analysis:

  • Remove the arrival-order cue and see whether FCFS survives and whether new tie-breaking conventions appear.
  • Train from scratch or from differently trained graduates to test whether cross-play compatibility is inherited.
  • Test the reverse rule at the T-junction (force turning cars first in early training) to see whether through-first is a true convention (stable either way) or kinematics (always drifts back).
  • Log $\lambda_i$ per operator in shared runs to test the "counterweight" explanation of why sharing prevents freezing.
  • Separate noise floor and budget scaling in the 0.20 noise-floor check to see which one reduces freezing.
  • Add pedestrians and human-like IDM cars to test robustness of the negotiation style with non-learning partners.
  • Make the observation set-based (attention over all neighbours instead of the 4 nearest) to remove the 4-car ceiling in dense traffic.

Part FPractice questions (with answers)

  1. Why does the study compare every behaviour with the school graduate and not just with 50%? Because every fleet starts from the graduate. Anything the graduate already did shows up in every fleet. Against 50%, 18 of 40 rule cells look like conventions; against the graduate, only 1 does.

  2. Write the safety cost of a car that is stationary at the side of an empty road. $c = 0$: no contact, on the road, no neighbour with TTC under 1.5 s, no PET event.

  3. A car holds TTC = 0.6 s to one neighbour for 5 steps. What does it pay in TTC cost? $0.1 \times (1 - 0.6/1.5) = 0.06$ per step, so $0.3$ in total.

  4. At what multiplier value do reward and cost advantages carry equal weight in Equation (3)? $\lambda = 1$. Every frozen run froze after the mean multiplier passed 1.

  5. Why is a per-step budget satisfied by standing still, and why would a per-km budget not be? Cost per step = cost per km × km per step, and km per step is 0 when stopped. Per km, a stopped car has no distance to divide by, so it cannot score as safe.

  6. Shared networks see 8 times the data. How did the study show that data is not the whole story? Shared PPO and MAPPO-L trained to 3.75M agent-steps (equal data per network) still beat Direct PPO at 30M: 79% vs 70% arrival. And Direct PPO at 240M steps reached only 72%.

  7. How did the study show that the central critic is not what prevents freezing? A shared network with a local critic and the same 1/2 budget met it in 20 of 21 runs, froze in none, and arrived in 89% of trips.

  8. Why is MAPPO-L's through-first result called a "candidate pattern"? It survives Holm correction only in a test that pools events. With 5 fleets per arm no fleet-level test could pass the correction, and the opposite rule was not tested.

  9. Why does FCFS appear without being learned in traffic? The first car often clears the zone before the second arrives, and the observation tells each car the neighbours' time to the conflict site. The graduate already does FCFS in 97% of crossing events.

  10. What does Lin's concordance of 0.96 between stuck-alone and stuck-in-traffic tell you? The cars stop just as often with no one around. The freezing is not caution towards other cars.

  11. Why can crashing be "cheap" under the Phase 1 reward, and how does Phase 2 fix it? There is no collision term, and a crash ends the stream of -0.05 time penalties. Phase 2 makes a crash pay $c_{\text{time}}(T - t_c)$, the time penalty of all the steps it skipped.

  12. What is the smallest possible p-value of an exact two-sided permutation test with 5 vs 5 fleets, and why? $\binom{10}{5} = 252$ splits; the two most extreme splits give $2/252 \approx 0.008$.

  13. Why does the paper use the geometric mean of crash ratios over scenes? Ratios are multiplicative (2 and 0.5 should cancel), and the geometric mean stops one extreme scene from dominating.

  14. Why does CoPO-style freeze without any budget? (Analysis) With 45° mixing a car earns 0.707 of the others' mean reward, so letting others pass pays, and its own credit is diluted. Since all cars share the network, they can all learn to yield, which ends in stand-offs.

  15. What does the shuffle test (Exp 6) show about the lidar encoders? Training on a fixed shuffle of beam order did not change FC (expected), and also did not change CNN or transformer reconstruction. So reconstruction quality alone does not show that an encoder uses the spatial layout of the beams.
